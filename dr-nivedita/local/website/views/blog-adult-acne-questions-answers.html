Adult Acne Q&A: Dr. Nivedita Dadu Answers the Questions You're Actually Asking

Acne isn't just a teenage problem anymore, and if you're dealing with breakouts well into your 20s, 30s, or even 40s despite doing "everything right," you're far from alone. If you've been researching Adult Acne treatment in Delhi for persistent adult acne, this Q&A pulls together the exact questions patients ask most often in clinic, answered directly by Dr. Nivedita Dadu.

Why do I always break out right before an important event?

This one's genuinely scientific, not just bad luck. Before any major event or function, stress and anxiety levels rise, which pushes cortisol levels up too. Higher cortisol makes skin produce more oil, pores clog more easily, and the odds of a pimple showing up right when you least want it go up significantly. It's a well-documented pattern, and unfortunately, there's no real way around it beyond managing stress and keeping a targeted pimple treatment on hand for exactly these moments.

Does diet actually affect acne?

Genuinely, yes, and diet plays a bigger role than most people realise. Sugar and junk food burgers, pizza, pasta, chocolate, even mangoes are well-known triggers. But dairy is the piece people consistently miss. Products made from cow's milk and buffalo milk paneer, ghee, cheese, and whey protein specifically are strongly linked to breakouts. A better alternative where possible is switching to plant-based milk like soy or almond milk.

The science behind this comes down to hormones. Cow and buffalo milk contain insulin-like growth factor, and consuming this hormone regularly significantly increases the likelihood of breakouts. It also drives excess oil production, which clogs pores further and compounds the problem.

What about lactose-free or A2 milk does that help?

This is a genuinely common misconception worth clearing up. Lactose-free milk and A2 milk are still derived from cow's milk, which means the same hormone insulin-like growth factor is still present. These options are genuinely helpful for people with digestion issues, inflammation sensitivity, or lactose intolerance specifically, but they won't reduce acne any more than regular dairy would, since the hormone responsible for triggering breakouts is still there.

Why is adult, hormonal acne becoming so common now?

Adult female jawline acne has become a genuinely common concern breakouts appearing after 25 or 30, concentrated specifically along the jawline. Hormonal acne tends to be quite painful, showing up as thick, nodular pimples that don't respond to standard pimple creams. This is very different from teenage acne, which typically appears on the cheeks, tends to be oilier-skin-related, and responds fairly easily to over-the-counter treatment.

Managing hormonal acne genuinely requires diet and lifestyle correction alongside it, and in many cases, oral medication becomes necessary to bring down the inflammation enough for the skin to actually settle.

Can emergency contraceptive pills trigger acne?

Yes, genuinely. Emergency contraceptives contain very high doses of hormones like levonorgestrel, and breakouts after taking one are a fairly common side effect worth being aware of.

Is isotretinoin safe to take, and can I take it on my own?

There's a lot of confusion around isotretinoin, so this needs to be said clearly it's a genuinely effective medication for acne, particularly for inflammatory, nodular, and cystic acne, adult acne, and cases that haven't responded to topical gels and creams. But it absolutely must be taken under a doctor's prescription, never self-administered.

A few critical precautions apply. You cannot conceive while on isotretinoin, and this restriction extends for a month after stopping it too, since it stays in the bloodstream that long. Blood donation is also off the table while on this medication. Before starting, your doctor will run baseline blood tests liver function, cholesterol, and lipid profile to confirm it's safe to begin. Anyone with a past history of depression needs closer monitoring while on isotretinoin as well. Used correctly and under proper supervision, though, the results are genuinely excellent, and there's no need to be afraid of it.

Can dry skin get acne too?

Yes, definitely this is a genuine misconception worth correcting. Acne isn't exclusive to oily skin. In fact, hormonal acne often shows up on drier skin, while classic teenage acne tends to appear on oilier skin. If your skin is dry and breaking out, using oily-skin products like salicylic acid face washes will only worsen the dryness. Matching your products to your actual skin type matters more here than most people realise.

Does icing or steaming help with acne?

No, extreme temperatures aren't genuinely good for skin, acne or otherwise. Icing and steaming have become trendy, with a lot of gadgets marketed around them, but if your skin is sensitive or prone to rosacea, icing can actually trigger flare-ups and increase sensitivity rather than help. Steam has one legitimate, limited use if you're trying to extract stubborn blackheads, brief steaming can loosen pores beforehand. But follow it up by closing pores again, and avoid overly hot steam or direct, prolonged ice application either way.

Do pimple patches actually work?

Yes, for isolated pimples, they genuinely help. Most pimple patches contain salicylic acid or hydrocolloid, which speeds up healing while also concealing the pimple so makeup goes on smoothly over it. That said, patches only make sense for one or two spots — if breakouts are widespread, you can't realistically patch your entire face, and oral medication or a full-face treatment cream becomes the more practical option instead.

Why do I only get acne on one side of my face?

This actually has a fairly simple explanation. If you consistently sleep on one side, that side sees more breakouts. Another common culprit is your phone people rarely clean their phone screens properly, and those screens carry significant bacterial buildup. Holding your phone against the same cheek repeatedly transfers that bacteria directly, which is exactly why one-sided acne often correlates with phone habits.

With so many actives available retinol, niacinamide, hyaluronic acid what should I actually use?

People increasingly rely on these trending actives and still show up in clinic wondering why their acne hasn't cleared despite using niacinamide, salicylic acid, and everything else that's easily available. Here's the honest answer for genuinely persistent acne, niacinamide and salicylic acid alone aren't enough. A reasonable maintenance routine might include niacinamide during the day, salicylic acid on alternate days or twice a week, and retinol at night for skin that's already fairly clear. But if your skin actually needs correction right now, you can't rely on these three alone.

For real correction, stronger molecules are needed benzoyl peroxide, adapalene, tretinoin, and similar options exist and genuinely work better for active, persistent acne. This might also mean oral antibiotics, deeper lifestyle changes, addressing dandruff if that's a contributing factor, or considering whether hormonal acne is the underlying driver. If you've been using salicylic acid and retinol for a long stretch without real improvement and still haven't stopped, it's worth recalibrating your expectations of what those specific ingredients alone can achieve.

Is there a favourite active for acne specifically?

Azelaic acid is a genuine favourite here. It has antibacterial action, reduces inflammation, fades both red and brown post-acne marks, and carries a lower risk of purging compared to some other actives. Overall, the results tend to be genuinely strong and well-tolerated.

Does washing your face more often prevent acne?

No, and this is a common misconception worth correcting directly. Twice a day is genuinely more than enough, using a face wash matched to your specific skin type. Oily skin does well with salicylic acid-based washes; for pigmentation concerns, malic acid or benzoyl peroxide face washes work well too, particularly for pus-filled breakouts. But if your skin is dry, these same products will only worsen the dryness.

Washing more frequently doesn't clean skin better it disrupts the skin's natural barrier, leading to more dehydration and damage. And once that barrier is compromised, bacterial infection risk actually goes up. There's also a rebound effect worth understanding stripping oil too aggressively signals to skin that it needs to produce even more oil to compensate, which works against the very goal of reducing breakouts.

Can scarring be prevented while acne is still active?

Yes, and this timing matters significantly. The more redness present during an active breakout, the higher the chances of scarring afterward. Using scar-prevention creams while pimples are still active and red rather than waiting until after genuinely helps. Allantoin-based and silicone-based creams work well here, and Centella Asiatica is a particularly strong molecule for this purpose, reducing inflammation and significantly lowering the odds of scarring later.

Once scarring has actually formed, it's permanent, and lasers become the only real solution at that point. Once redness has settled and skin is no longer sensitive, tretinoin-based creams become a useful option too they exfoliate, stimulate collagen production, and help superficial scars and textural concerns heal over time. Tretinoin isn't safe during pregnancy, breastfeeding, or while planning to conceive, so that consideration applies here as well.
